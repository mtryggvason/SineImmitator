    Why sine waves?

    Sound is air pressure moving back and forth. In the early 1800s Joseph Fourier showed that any such signal can be rebuilt by adding up sine waves, each with its own frequency, loudness and timing. So in principle every sound can be played with sine waves: a voice, a violin or a slammed door.

    Pitched sounds need only a few. A sung note or a whistle is mostly one base frequency (the fundamental) plus harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4×… that frequency. The spacing gives the pitch and the relative loudness of each harmonic gives the timbre, so a handful of sines already sounds close.

    Noisy sounds need a lot. An “s”, a breath or a cymbal spreads its energy over thousands of frequencies with no stable peaks. That’s why they come out warbly here: this app uses at most 64 sines.

    How this app does it. While a recording plays, it splits the sound into its frequencies (a Fourier transform) many times a second, picks the strongest peaks and steers a set of sine oscillators to follow them. On the screen above, the white dots are the recording’s spectrum and the red columns are the sines being played.
